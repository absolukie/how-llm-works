How a large
language model works

A language model does one trick: it guesses the next word. To pull it off, it turns your words into numbers, runs them through a stack of identical blocks that mix in context, then rolls weighted dice to pick what comes next — one word at a time, hundreds of times per answer. Tap any stage below and play with the working toys.

↓ data flows down

Computers can't read letters — only numbers. So the first thing any language model does is chop your text into pieces called tokens: whole words, word chunks, sometimes single letters. Each piece gets an ID number. From this point on, the model never sees your words — only the numbers.

AnalogyThink of it like… cutting a sentence into Scrabble tiles. Common letter-groups like un or ing earn their own printed tile; rare words get spelled out letter by letter. Either way, every tile gets a number stamped on the back.

Why chop text at all? Because the model only speaks numbers — and the vocabulary size is a tradeoff. Too few tiles and every sentence becomes a long crawl of letters; too many and the final layer, which scores every tile, gets enormous and slow. Real vocabularies land around 100,000–200,000 tiles: the compromise between short sequences and a manageable output layer.

The standard recipe for learning those tiles is BPE — byte-pair encoding: start from single characters, repeatedly merge the most frequent neighboring pair, stop at the target size. The playground below runs the genuine algorithm, trained live on a toy corpus in your browser.

What do the little numbers mean — like the 41 stamped on “un”? Each one is the tile's address in the vocabulary: one giant numbered list of every tile the model knows. “un” is entry #41, so your text reaches the model as the number 41 — it never sees the letters u-n at all. The numbers themselves are arbitrary (another model's “un” might be #9,204); what matters is that words become a list of numbers the model can compute with. In the next stage, each number gets traded in for its starting meaning.

Why should you care how text gets chopped? Three ways it bites in real life. The model can’t truly see spelling — “the” is one tile but “teh” is three, so a typo looks like a different word entirely. It can’t do arithmetic — numbers arrive as digit confetti, never as values. And other languages pay more — English gets roughly a tile per word while Chinese often costs a tile per character, making the same meaning longer, slower, and pricier to process.

Takeaway“The model never sees words — only numbered tiles. ‘Unbelievable’ is tile 41 + tile 51 to the model, and it has no idea what either ‘means’ yet.”

Common misconceptionMost people think the model reads text like we do — actually “the” is a single tile while the typo “teh” shatters into three, so a one-letter slip looks like a completely different input.

Most tokenizers learn their vocabulary with byte-pair encoding: start from single characters and repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair, until the vocabulary hits its target size — tens of thousands of pieces. Frequent words earn their own token; rare words get spelled out in chunks, so any text can be represented. Rule of thumb: about 4 characters ≈ 1 token in English. The tokenizer is frozen before training and shared by the model forever — which is why a model can never learn a new word's spelling after the fact.

Each token's ID is swapped for a long list of numbers — its embedding. Think of those numbers as coordinates on a map: words with similar meanings end up as neighbors. King lands near queen, far from carburetor. Then every vector gets stamped with its position — because dog bites man and man bites dog use the same words in a different order, and order changes everything.

AnalogyThink of it like… giving every Scrabble tile a personality, then placing it on a giant map where similar meanings live in the same neighborhood. And like seat numbers at a dinner table, each tile is also stamped with where it sits — move the seats, change the conversation.

The lookup table is the embedding matrix — vocabulary-size × model-width, thousands of numbers per token in real models — and it is learned during training: words appearing in similar contexts get pushed together. Position used to be added as fixed sine waves or learned offsets; modern models bake rotation into the attention step itself (RoPE) — and stretch it to huge contexts with rescaling tricks like YaRN, one way open models reach quarter-million-token windows. The resulting vector joins the residual stream: one vector per token that flows through the entire network, refined at every block.

Takeaway“Meaning is geometry: words that behave alike live in the same neighborhood of a thousand-dimensional map.”

Common misconceptionMost people think each word gets a fixed definition — actually a word’s vector is just a starting address; its meaning gets rewritten by every block that follows. And order isn’t built in — position has to be stamped on.

This is where the understanding gets built. Your vectors march through a tall stack of identical transformer blocks — 96 of them in GPT-3. In each block, tokens trade information with each other, then every vector gets polished on its own. With every block, each word is understood a little more in context — by the last block, bank next to river and bank next to money have become two completely different vectors. Open each part:

AnalogyThink of it like… an editorial pass. Each block takes every word's sticky note, lets the words compare notes with each other, then rewrites each note a little smarter. Run 96 passes and the notes get very wise.

The cleverest part of the machine. Every token asks three questions: what am I looking for? (query), what do I offer? (key), and what will I share if chosen? (value). Each token compares its query against everyone else's key — good match, strong link — then mixes in the winners' values. In the cat licked its paw, the word its goes looking for something that could own a paw, and finds cat. Tap through the examples, then try the live heatmap.

AnalogyThink of it like… a group chat. Before anyone speaks, each person decides who to listen to: the pronoun checks with the noun, the verb checks with its subject. Attention is just that — every word choosing whose input matters most.

For input matrix X, each head computes Q=XWQ, K=XWK, V=XWV; scores = QKT/√dk; a causal mask blanks out future positions — a token may only attend to the left, which is what makes step-by-step generation possible; softmax turns each row of scores into weights; output = weights·V. The pairwise comparison is why attention costs grow with the square of sequence length — the famous O(n²) bottleneck, and the reason frontier labs now compress it: latent attention (MLA) or sparse attention that skips most of the distant past, unlocking million-token contexts.

Takeaway“Every word re-reads the whole sentence and decides who to listen to before it speaks — ‘it’ in ‘the cat licked its paw’ is mostly listening to ‘cat’.”

Common misconceptionMost people think the model reads left-to-right like we do — actually every token compares itself against all previous tokens at once, in parallel. The ‘reading order’ is an illusion; the comparison is simultaneous.

One attention pattern isn't enough, so each block runs many in parallel — called heads. One head might link pronouns to nouns, another might track verb tense, a third might watch punctuation. Their outputs are glued back together into a single vector.

Each head gets its own WQ, WK, WV, projecting to a smaller width dk = dmodel/h (GPT-3: 96 heads × 128 dims each). All heads run simultaneously on the same input; their results are concatenated and re-projected with WO. Most modern models also share key/value heads across query groups (grouped-query attention), shrinking the memory cache several-fold for nearly free. Heads genuinely specialize during training — researchers have found heads that do nothing but resolve pronouns or match opening brackets to closing ones.

Attention mixes information between tokens; the feed-forward network works on each token alone. It's a tiny two-layer neural net applied to every position identically — and it's where most of the model's factual “knowledge” lives, as millions of learned associations.

FFN(x) = W2·SwiGLU(W1x) in most modern models: the vector is expanded to a few times its width, passed through a gated nonlinearity, and squeezed back down. Roughly two-thirds of a transformer's parameters sit in these layers. A useful mental image: the first matrix detects patterns (“this looks like a capital-city context”), the second writes the conclusion back into the vector.

Each sub-step is wrapped in two safety rails. The residual connection adds the input back to the output — information can never be destroyed, only refined. Layer normalization rescales the numbers to keep them well-behaved across a hundred layers.

Modern blocks use pre-norm: x + Sublayer(LayerNorm(x)). Residuals give gradients a clean highway back through all N blocks — that's what makes training 100-layer networks possible at all; without them the signal would vanish or explode. Normalization keeps every activation in a range the next layer expects, stabilizing the whole stack.

Every block has the same two moves — multi-head self-attention, then the feed-forward net — each in a residual + normalization sandwich. Blocks share a shape but not weights, so early blocks tend to handle surface patterns (word shape, grammar) while later blocks handle abstract ones (logic, long-range references). Nearly all of the model's parameters — and nearly all the compute — live in this stack.

One more 2024–2026 twist: the biggest models are mixture-of-experts. Each block's feed-forward net is split into dozens or hundreds of specialist sub-networks (“experts”), and a tiny router wakes only a handful per token — plus one shared expert that always runs. Moonshot's open-weight Kimi K2 reports about a trillion total parameters with only ~32B active per token; DeepSeek-V4-Pro reports ~1.6T total with ~49B active. Parameters scale up; the bill per token barely moves.

Takeaway“Each transformer block is an editorial pass that rewrites every word’s sticky note with more context — ‘bank’ becomes a different word near ‘river’ than near ‘money’.”

Common misconceptionMost people think the model ‘understands’ a sentence in one go — actually comprehension is refined gradually: early blocks handle spelling and grammar, later blocks handle logic and long-range references. There is no single moment of understanding.

After the last block, the model takes the final vector — the one holding the whole sentence's meaning — and scores every word it knows as a candidate for what comes next. Each raw score is called a logit. Plain English: the model turns a volume knob for every word in its vocabulary at once. Most knobs sit near zero; a few get cranked high.

AnalogyThink of it like… a talent-show scorecard. Every word in the dictionary gets judged on one question — “how likely are you to come next?” — and most score near zero while a handful score high.

This is the unembedding: a dmodel × vocabulary matrix — sometimes literally the same weights as the input embedding, tied. One matrix multiply turns one vector into ~100k scores. During training this happens at every position at once (each position predicts the token after it); during generation, only the last position matters.

Those raw scores are hard to read: they can be negative, and they don't add up to anything. Softmax translates them into proper percentages that sum to 100%. It also exaggerates — a word scoring just a little higher can end up a lot more likely. What comes out is the model's honest uncertainty about what comes next.

AnalogyThink of it like… slicing a pie. The raw scores decide how big each slice is, and softmax guarantees the slices always add up to exactly one whole pie: 100%.

pi = ezi / Σj ezj. Because of the exponential, the top few tokens usually hog nearly all the probability while the long tail shares crumbs. Softmax has no learned parameters — it's pure arithmetic, and it's the same formula used inside the attention step back in stage 3.

Takeaway“The model doesn’t pick a word — it scores every word it knows, then slices the scores into a pie that always sums to 100%.”

Common misconceptionMost people think there’s a ‘right answer’ slot the model fills — actually it produces a full ranking over 100,000+ words at every step; the ‘answer’ is just whichever slice the dice lands on.

The model doesn't have to pick the top word. Instead it rolls weighted dice over the percentages. Temperature is the knob: turn it down and the favorite almost always wins — focused, but repetitive. Turn it up and long shots get real chances — creative, but weird. Top-k and top-p first cross the strangest options off the list entirely. Same prompt, different rolls, different answers — which is why asking twice gives you two replies.

AnalogyThink of it like… a roulette wheel with slices sized by probability. Temperature is how adventurous the spin is: ice-cold and the ball nearly always lands on the biggest slice; hot and it can land almost anywhere.

Temperature T rescales the logits before softmax: pi ∝ ezi/T. As T→0 this approaches argmax — greedy decoding, always the top token; T=1 leaves probabilities untouched; T>1 flattens them. Top-k keeps only the k highest-scoring tokens; top-p (nucleus sampling) keeps the smallest set whose probabilities sum to p. Chatbots typically sample around T≈0.7 with top-p≈0.9: lively, but the truly weird options never get a ticket. Reasoning models add one more knob — thinking effort (low → max in current APIs): how many tokens the model may spend reasoning before it answers. More thinking, better answers, bigger bill — inference-time compute as a dial, not a fixed cost.

Takeaway“Temperature doesn’t make the model smarter or dumber — it only changes how adventurous the dice roll is.”

Common misconceptionMost people think a low temperature is ‘more correct’ — actually it just makes the model more repetitive. Correctness comes from training, not temperature; turn it to zero and you get the most likely answer, not the truest one.

The winning word gets glued onto the end of your text — and the whole pipeline runs again. And again. Until the model plays a special stop word. It never “writes a paragraph”; it predicts one next word, hundreds of times in a row. That loop has a name — autoregressive generation — which just means each step's output becomes the next step's input.

AnalogyThink of it like… a relay race where each runner hands the baton to the next version of themselves. One word per lap, hundreds of laps per answer — and the baton is everything said so far.

AnalogyThink of it like… keeping your notes instead of re-reading the whole book for every new page. The model saves each word's keys and values after computing them once (the KV cache), so every new step only does fresh math for the newcomer.

Recomputing everything each step would be quadratic; the KV cache avoids it by saving every token's keys and values after its first pass, so each new step only computes the newcomer. The context window is how many tokens the model can juggle at once — a few thousand in early models, a million in current frontier ones, afforded by the compressed-attention tricks from stage 3; past the limit, the oldest tokens fall off. Generation halts on a learned end-of-text token or a length cap. And the loop no longer runs alone: modern systems call tools mid-generation — running code, searching the web, checking a calculator — which multiplies what the raw next-token engine can do without changing its weights.

Takeaway“The model writes one tile at a time and can never take one back — every sentence is a one-way trip.”

Common misconceptionMost people think the model plans a sentence and then writes it — actually it plans nothing; each tile is chosen with only the past to lean on. The KV cache is just its notebook, so it doesn’t re-read the whole book for every new word.

How it learned

Everything above is the performance. This is the rehearsal. Nobody programmed any of it — every number in the machine started as random noise, and training shaped them in four phases. Same prompt, four different behaviors:

The model reads an enormous slice of the internet — books, articles, code, forums — with a single job: guess the next word, trillions of times. Every wrong guess nudges its billions of numbers a hair in the right direction. Grammar, facts, even step-by-step reasoning show up on their own — side effects of getting extremely good at next-word prediction.

AnalogyThink of it like… school. Read the whole library, take ten trillion pop quizzes (guess the next word), and knowledge soaks in as a side effect of all that practice.

This is self-supervised learning: the data labels itself, because the next word is always right there in the text — no human annotation needed. The loss is cross-entropy between the predicted and actual next token. Scale is the story: frontier runs now train on tens of trillions of tokens — open labs report 15T+ for Kimi K2, 32T+ for DeepSeek-V4-Pro — with up to trillions of parameters, costing millions of dollars in compute — and capabilities nobody explicitly taught tend to emerge past certain scales.

Takeaway“Nobody taught the model a single fact — it absorbed grammar, facts, and reasoning as side effects of guessing the next word trillions of times.”

Common misconceptionMost people think the model looks up facts in a database — actually everything it ‘knows’ is smeared across billions of weights. There is no fact shelf to open.

A pretrained model is a brilliant mimic with no manners — ask it a question and it might reply with five more questions. Fine-tuning trains it further on curated examples: instructions paired with good responses. It learns the shape of being helpful — answer the question, follow the format, hold the tone.

AnalogyThink of it like… an apprenticeship. School gave you knowledge; now you shadow a master and learn the craft — how to actually be useful to someone.

Usually supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on tens of thousands of high-quality demonstrations. Same next-token objective, new data distribution. It's cheap next to pretraining — hours to days — but it decisively steers behavior. Since 2025 the recipe has partly flipped: labs now grow behaviors with reinforcement learning first (next stage), then use SFT to distill the best of those behaviors back into the model. Most of what you experience as “the assistant's personality” is still set here, not in pretraining.

Takeaway“Pretraining teaches the model how the world talks; fine-tuning teaches it how to be helpful.”

Common misconceptionMost people think fine-tuning adds knowledge — actually it mostly teaches manners and shape: answer the question, follow the format. The knowledge was already there; fine-tuning just teaches it how to act.

Some answers can be checked — a math proof, a program that runs, a tool call that succeeds. So the lab lets the model attempt thousands of problems, rewards the tries that get verifiably right answers, and reinforces the long chains of thought that led there. Nobody writes out the reasoning for it; the model discovers working thought patterns through trial, error, and a scoreboard. This is the breakthrough behind every “thinking” model since late 2024, starting with OpenAI's o1-preview (September 2024).

AnalogyThink of it like… a coach with a whistle and a scoreboard. Run the play thousands of times; the attempts that score get repeated until the thinking itself gets sharper.

The technique is RLVR — reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. No human judges, no learned reward model: the reward is “did the code pass its tests?” Variants of the GRPO algorithm update the model toward attempts that beat the average of their group. The usual recipe: a little supervised fine-tuning on long reasoning examples to cold-start the behavior, then large-scale RL, then distilling the best traces back with SFT. The newest models even blend thinking and non-thinking in one set of weights, with reasoning switched on or off per request.

Takeaway“The model learned to think by getting rewarded for right answers — nobody ever showed it how to reason.”

Common misconceptionMost people think ‘thinking’ models were taught logic step by step — actually they discovered working thought patterns through trial and error against checkable answers. The reasoning was never written down by a human.

Some things can't be checked with a scoreboard — taste, tone, honesty, safety. Here humans rank several candidate answers from best to worst, and the model is tuned toward what people prefer. This is the layer that teaches it to refuse harmful requests, admit when it's unsure, and generally act like the assistant you actually want to talk to.

AnalogyThink of it like… a manners coach. Kindness, taste, honesty — none of these can be scored automatically, so humans rank your answers and you learn what people prefer.

RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback (modern variants like DPO skip the RL and optimize preferences directly). The model is nudged, not rewritten, toward preferred outputs, with a penalty for drifting too far from its tuned self (the KL constraint) — which keeps it from gaming the reward model into nonsense. In the 2026 recipe this stage handles preference — style, safety, helpfulness — while raw reasoning ability increasingly comes from the verifiable-reward stage before it. Alignment is steering, not a guarantee: it's why the model usually refuses, not a proof that it always will.

Takeaway“Taste can’t be computed, so humans ranked answers and the model learned what people prefer — alignment is steering, not a guarantee.”

Common misconceptionMost people think RLHF makes the model safe and honest — actually it teaches the model what humans reward, which is not the same as true. It’s why the model usually refuses, not a proof it always will.

Takeaway“Pretraining teaches the model how the world talks; fine-tuning teaches it how to be helpful; RL teaches it manners and persistence.”

Common misconceptionMost people think there’s one training event — actually it’s four jobs in sequence: absorb the world, learn the assistant’s shape, practice thinking against a scoreboard, then learn human taste. Skip any layer and you get a different creature.

Takeaway“An LLM is a next-tile predictor wearing a very convincing understanding costume — and the costume is the product.”

Common misconceptionMost people think fluency means comprehension — actually the model is fluent because it mastered the statistics of how humans sound, not because it checked anything against reality. Treat it like a brilliant improviser, not a database.
